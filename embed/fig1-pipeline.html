Six decisions behind every curve score

Every published score for a digitized line or scatter plot is a sequence of six decisions. Benchmarks differ less in their arithmetic than in which option they take at each step. Pick a benchmark to see its path through the six cards; the sketches redraw to show its choice.

Figure 1. Each card is one decision; the chips are the options found in the literature and the highlighted chip is the selected benchmark’s choice. The sketches use the same synthetic curve throughout: a peak on a sloping baseline (blue) and a digitization shifted by 4% of the x-range (orange).

What to try

  • Click CHART-Info 6b and then FPC-NRMSE. Both pair series one-to-one and measure vertical differences, but one samples at the ground-truth points and divides by the value, the other samples every pixel column and divides by the y-range; and only one of them penalizes a curve that stops early.
  • Compare Scatteract with CHART-Info scatter: the same point sets, but a fixed 2% circle on one side and a covariance-scaled distance with an assignment step on the other.
  • Click RMS, Adaptive MAPE and TBE in turn. They share the table key-value matching and differ only in card 4, the normalizer, which is the whole argument of the 2026 papers that introduced the last two.
  • Click ECS and Fréchet: the two order-aware methods, which are the only ones here that work for curves that are not functions of x.

How it is computed

A curve score is a composition: pair → sample → measure → normalize → aggregate, with a rule for missing data applied at the pairing or sampling step. Most benchmarks take the same option at step 1 (one-to-one assignment, normalized by max(N_GT, N_pred)) and at step 3 (a vertical difference for lines, a Euclidean or Mahalanobis distance for points). They differ mainly at steps 2, 4 and 6, and that is where the figures that follow spend their time.

Notes on this implementation

  • The chips name the options found in the literature reviewed for this collection; a benchmark’s chip is the option its paper or scorer describes, simplified to one phrase. Card 1 for RMS, Adaptive MAPE and TBE reads “by table key” because those metrics match cells by their headers before comparing values.
  • Plot2Spectra’s “hit rate” is a threshold on the mean pixel distance of a whole line, not on single points.
  • In card 4 the two example misses are both 0.8 units on a 0 to 10 axis; the labels show what each normalizer makes of them.
